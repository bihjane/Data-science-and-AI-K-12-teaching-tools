# Word Detectives: Who Wrote It? (ages 14-18)
**Intelligent Futures · Free Trial Lesson** · From the course *Data Explorers for Schools*

**Grade band:** Ages 5-18 · Science, humanities, languages, health, business and primary  
**Time:** One or two 50-minute periods  
**Standards:** Reading for evidence; data literacy

## Why students need the math
When two writers both claim a text, word habits can settle it. The classic case is the disputed Federalist Papers (Mosteller & Wallace, 1964); the same method works for any two authors in any language.

## Learning objectives
- Use word frequencies as evidence of authorship
- Explain how a computer can classify text

## Data
- Public-domain texts by two authors (Project Gutenberg, Wikisource), for example the Federalist Papers or two writers in your own language

## Free tools
- Voyant Tools
- Google Sheets

## Lesson flow
1. Paste five texts by each author into Voyant Tools.
2. Record how often each uses a few common function words, per 1,000 words, in Sheets.
3. Compare the disputed text to both profiles and make a claim.
4. Discuss: this is a classifier; what are its features?

## Student handout
1. Choose two authors and one disputed or unsigned text (or use the classic Federalist Papers case).
2. Paste five texts by Author A into Voyant. Record the rate of 4-5 common function words per 1,000 words.
3. Do the same for Author B.
4. Now analyze the disputed text. Which author does it resemble?
5. Explain how this is a classification model. What are the features? What are the labels?
6. What are the limits of using word counts as evidence?

## Exit ticket
Write a claim-evidence-reasoning paragraph on authorship.

## Teacher notes & answer key
- Function words (the, of, upon, while) are good fingerprints because writers use them without thinking.
- Classic case: Hamilton used 'upon' often and Madison rarely did; Madison used 'whilst'. Federalist No. 51 matches Madison, as Mosteller & Wallace (1964) concluded.
- Features = word rates; labels = author; the 'model' compares a new essay to each author's profile.
- Limits: small samples, editing by others, topic effects on word choice.

## Extensions
- Use Google Sheets to compute the distance from No. 51 to each author's average profile (a nearest-centroid classifier).
- Try the method with two writers in your own language.
